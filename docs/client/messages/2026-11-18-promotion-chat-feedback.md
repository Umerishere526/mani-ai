## The chat (Directive)

mani: Hi user1, good to see you again. How would you like me to speak with you today?

User: Directive

mani: How can I help you today?

User: i feel like everyone around me is moving forward in life, and I'm the only one standing still.

mani: That comparison can be brutal. It makes your own life look small while everyone else's looks like it's moving.
But standing still and falling behind are not the same thing. Standing still means you haven't changed direction. It
doesn't mean you haven't been trying, and it doesn't mean you're behind. I want to understand what you're comparing
yourself against. When you say everyone is moving forward, what is it specifically that makes you feel like you're the
only one standing still?

User: Honestly, I don't even know anymore. My manager keeps telling me I'm doing a great job, but when promotion
opportunities come up, someone else always gets picked. It's getting harder to believe that my hard work actually
means anything.

mani: That is a hard thing to sit with. Your manager says you're doing well, and then someone else gets the
promotion. So the praise stops feeling like good news and starts feeling like something that doesn't lead anywhere.
It is not really about whether you work hard. It is about whether your work actually counts for something. When you
watch someone else get promoted, what hurts most: that they were chosen over you, that you were left out of the
decision, or that your hard work seems to have made no difference?

User: I think it's that I'm starting to feel invisible. I see people who joined after me getting opportunities, and I
keep wondering what they have that I don't. Maybe I'm just not someone people see as leadership material.

mani: That is the part that hurts most, isn't it. Not just missing a promotion, but feeling like people look past
you. And the comparison makes it worse. People who joined after you are getting the opportunities, so it starts to
feel like something about you is missing. Something they can see and you can't. But being passed over is not the same
as being invisible. It is also not proof that you are not leadership material. It is evidence about how decisions
have gone, not about what you are made of. I want to understand what you think they have that you don't.

User: I think they're more confident. They speak up in meetings, they know how to present themselves, and they seem
comfortable making decisions. I'm more reserved. I usually just focus on getting the work done. Maybe being good at my
job isn't enough to get ahead.

mani: That is a clear belief forming: doing the work well is not enough, and the people who get ahead have a kind of
presence you do not have. Being reserved is not the same as lacking leadership material. Plenty of strong leaders are
quiet. But right now you are comparing your inside with everyone else's outside. You see their confidence in the
room. You do not see the nerves, the preparation, or the meetings where they also stayed quiet. Your work is visible
to you because you are the one doing it. Their confidence is visible to you because it is loud. That does not mean
they have more of what it takes. It means they are easier to notice. What would it look like to you if being good at
your job was enough?

User: I guess I'd want my manager to notice the work without me having to constantly advertise myself. I don't want to
become someone who talks over everyone or takes credit for things. I just want to feel that doing good work actually
matters. And honestly, I'm tired of having to prove myself every single year.

mani: That is the core of it. You are not asking to become loud. You are tired of having your work speak for you and
still not being seen. And there is a real cost to that. Every year you put in the effort, wait for it to be noticed,
and then watch someone else move forward. It starts to feel like the effort itself is pointless. But there is a
difference between not advertising yourself and being invisible. Reserved people often do the work without making it
visible, then assume others can see it. Your manager may be seeing the output without seeing the judgement, the
decisions, and the effort behind it. That does not mean you have to become someone who talks over people. It means the
belief that good work alone should be enough is worth looking at, because right now it is costing you a lot. How
could you test whether that belief holds, without having to become a different person?

User: Not sure

mani: (the five steps of ABCDE, then) Ready to dive into a real life situation?

User: Start ABCDE

mani: A is for Activating Event. The situation is clear: you do good work, your manager tells you so, and then
someone else gets the promotion. People who joined after you are getting opportunities while you keep having to prove
yourself each year. Let's pin down the specific moment that set this off. Think of the most recent promotion round.
What happened, and what did you notice that made you feel invisible?

User: About two months ago, my manager announced that Sarah was getting promoted to team lead. She's been here for
barely two years. I've been here for six. I trained her when she joined, and now she's going to be leading projects
that I helped her learn how to handle. Nobody even discussed my name for the position.

mani: That is a clear activating event. You trained Sarah, she is now leading work you helped her learn, and your
name was never part of the conversation. The part that likely stings most is not that Sarah got it. It is that six
years of your effort did not even put you in the room where it was decided. Before we look at what you made of this,
one more thing. When the announcement happened, what did you do?

User: I congratulated her and acted like everything was fine. I didn't want to look bitter or jealous. But honestly, I
felt humiliated. I went back to my desk and barely spoke to anyone for the rest of the day. I haven't brought it up
with my manager because I don't even know how to have that conversation anymore.

mani: You did what you thought was expected of you. Congratulating her and keeping your reaction hidden protected you
from looking bitter, but it also meant nobody saw the humiliation you were carrying. And not raising it with your
manager has left you carrying this alone for two months. That silence probably reinforced the feeling that you are
invisible, because the one conversation that might have challenged it never happened. B is for Belief. Now let's look
at what you told yourself in that moment. What did you conclude about yourself or your work when your name was never
mentioned?

User: I think I told myself that I'm just not good enough. Maybe I'm reliable and hardworking, but I'm not someone
they see as special or capable of leading. And if someone I trained can move ahead of me so quickly, maybe that says
something about where I stand.

mani: That is the belief, and it is a heavy one. You took one decision that excluded you and turned it into a verdict
about your whole value: reliable and hardworking, but not special, not capable of leading. The part about Sarah stings
because it feels like evidence. If someone you trained can move ahead that fast, it seems to say something about where
you stand. But it does not say that. It says one role went to one person, and your name was not discussed. That is a
gap in what you know, not proof about you. C is for Consequences. So let's look at what this belief has been doing
since that day. What has it changed about how you work, how you show up, or how you feel about being there?

User: Honestly, I've stopped going above and beyond. I still do my job, but I don't volunteer for extra projects
anymore. What's the point? I used to get excited about proving myself, but now I just do what's expected and go home.
I also find myself avoiding Sarah a little, which makes me feel terrible because she hasn't actually done anything
wrong.

mani: That is a clear consequence. The belief has cost you the part of the job you used to enjoy, and it has put
distance between you and someone you respect. Stopping the extra work is understandable. When effort feels like it
does not count, pulling back feels like self-protection. But the avoidance of Sarah is the part worth noticing. You are
treating her as the person who took what was yours, even though she did not choose to take it. That guilt tells you it
is not really about her. D is for Dispute. Let's look at the belief itself. You said you are not good enough and not
someone they see as capable of leading. What makes that belief feel true, and is there anything you know that does not
fit it?

User: Well, I've never actually been told I'm bad at leading. My manager has trusted me to train new employees, handle
difficult projects, and make decisions when he's away. People come to me when they need help. But none of that seems
to translate into a promotion, so I don't know what to make of it.

mani: That is the tension right there. The evidence says people already trust you with leadership work. The promotion
says your name was not discussed. Both are true, and the second one does not cancel the first. What you know supports
the belief: six years of effort, one promotion round, and no conversation about you. What complicates it: your manager
has trusted you with difficult projects, decisions, and training people who are now moving up. If you were not seen
as capable, that trust would be hard to explain. So the belief may be accurate about one decision and still wrong
about you. The promotion shows a gap in how decisions are made, not a verdict on your ability. E is for Effective New
Belief. Based on everything you have looked at, what is a more balanced way of seeing this that you could actually
believe?

User: Maybe I'm not incapable of leading. I know I've handled responsibilities that require leadership. But I think
I've been assuming that if I just keep working hard, someone will eventually notice. Maybe that's not how it works
here. I still feel disappointed, and honestly, I'm not sure I trust my manager anymore.

mani: That is a real shift. You are no longer saying "I am not leadership material." You are saying the opposite is
true, and that you had been waiting for a system that rewards silent effort to notice you on its own. The
disappointment makes sense. Believing you are not capable would have been painful, but it would have been simple. Now
you are holding something harder: you may be capable, and still have been overlooked. That is easier to say and harder
to live with. Not trusting your manager is a reasonable conclusion from what happened. Your name was never discussed,
and you have no evidence that he advocates for you. That is worth taking seriously. How are you feeling about this
now, compared to two months ago?

User: Stronger

mani: You came in feeling invisible and stuck, and now you can see that your work has not been valued the way it
should have been, without turning that into a verdict on your ability. That is a stronger place to stand from.

## The client's review, reply by reply

1. **First reply.** Useful. Mani must not reinforce the negative self-assessment, and must not reassure early ("You're
   clearly good at what you do") without the information. Next: find out whether there was negative feedback, unclear
   promotion criteria, or a lack of recognition.
2. **"It is not really about whether you work hard..."** Too definitive: it tells them what the problem is instead of
   exploring it. Softer: "Maybe what's becoming frustrating is that all the effort you're putting in doesn't seem to be
   translating into anything meaningful for your career." The three-option question overlaps and is restrictive; an
   open question gives room. Watch for repetition next turn.
3. **"That is the part that hurts most, isn't it."** Reassurance too early ("not proof that you are not leadership
   material"): explore the evidence behind the belief first. "Something they can see and you can't" risks
   reinforcing the inadequacy story. The repeated probing (what hurts most, what do they have, what makes you feel
   this) reads as an assessment.
4. **"That is a clear belief forming..."** Too much explanation before the question: four interpretations in a row
   sound like a motivational coach. The reassurance is repeating. Explore whether they want more visibility or feel
   pushed into a personality that isn't theirs. The question ("What would it look like...") is abstract; make it
   concrete.
5. **"That is the core of it."** Too conclusive; leave room for correction ("Maybe what you're really wanting is to
   know that your work matters without having to constantly draw attention to yourself."). Still repetitive. "How
   could you test whether that belief holds" puts the solution on them; Mani should be ready to help build a practical
   experiment, such as a conversation with the manager about promotion criteria.
6. **"A is for Activating Event."** Abrupt: a small bridge first ("Given how many times this has happened, I think it
   might help to look closely at one particular experience rather than keep revisiting the whole pattern."). The event
   is overloaded: the most recent promotion decision should be the focus. Simplify the question to "What happened
   during the most recent promotion round?" Keep the thread of their distrust of the manager.
7. **"That is a clear activating event."** "The part that likely stings most is not that Sarah got it" assumes; softer:
   "Having trained her and then watching her move into that position must bring up some complicated feelings." Give
   the "what did you do" question context ("I'm also curious how you handled it in the moment..."). Keep the event
   apart from the interpretations.
8. **"You did what you thought was expected of you."** The biggest concern: "That silence probably reinforced the
   feeling" is an unsupported causal claim that suggests their silence contributed. Prefer "Since then, you've been
   carrying the disappointment without getting any clearer answers from your manager." Less interpretation. "B is for
   Belief." is instructional; smoother: "Let's look at what that experience led you to believe about yourself and your
   work."
9. **"That is the belief, and it is a heavy one."** Disputes too early ("But it does not say that"): D is the place.
   Repetitive. One area per question: "Since that promotion, have you noticed yourself approaching work any
   differently?"
10. **"That is a clear consequence."** "You are treating her as the person who took what was yours" is too definitive
    and implies the role was theirs; prefer "Part of you may be associating Sarah with the disappointment, even though
    you know she wasn't responsible for the decision." Acknowledge the loss of enthusiasm ("I used to get excited").
    The dispute question is textbook; more conversational: "When you look beyond this promotion decision, what
    experiences make you question your ability to lead? Have there also been moments when people trusted you with that
    kind of responsibility?"
11. **"That is the tension right there."** Too confident: being trusted with responsibilities is not promotion
    readiness. Don't turn every workplace problem into a distortion: unclear criteria, a manager not advocating, and
    unrecognised leadership work are real and need addressing. A new belief won't change the manager's decisions.
12. **"That is a real shift."** "You have no evidence that he advocates for you" is too strong; prefer "You haven't
    seen evidence that he's actively advocating for your progression." Emotional progress is not problem resolution:
    criteria, trust and their future at the company remain. After "feel better", see whether they want a practical
    next step.

## muhammad's answers (2026-11-18)

- Fewer points per reply, an open question instead of a pick-from-three, and step A as one specific event: Directive
  only. Supportive and Reflective stay as they are.
- No arguing with the belief before D: inside the set; the chat may still offer a fair perspective.
- After the feelings answer, offer a practical next step: yes.
- Readings as possibilities, each point once, a bridge before the first line, and eval cases: agreed with the design.
- Directive length: remove the contradicting lines first (option 1); a code check only if that fails.
