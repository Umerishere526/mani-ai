Thanksgiving Conversation and Framework Feedback

Overall Assessment

I found the ABCDE framework itself very enjoyable. Once we got into the exercise, I was engaged, the questions became meaningful, and there were moments when MANI helped me see things differently.

This is real progress, and I want us to preserve what is working.

However, outside the framework, MANI continues to struggle with three fundamental conversational abilities:

Listening: MANI frequently fails to understand what I am actually saying and makes assumptions about what I mean.

Following the conversation: When the conversation changes direction, MANI often fails to follow. She continues pursuing her own line of questioning rather than responding to where I want to go.

Memory and context: MANI forgets or misuses information I have already provided, repeats questions, and returns to subjects we have moved away from.

These issues significantly affect the experience because a good conversation depends on the ability to listen, understand, remember, and respond appropriately.

1. MANI Did Not Listen Carefully From the Beginning

I explained that Thanksgiving was coming up and that I would be spending it alone.

MANI responded:

"What was the day actually like for you, from the start?"

Thanksgiving had not happened yet.

I had to correct her by saying:

"It's this Thursday. It didn't happen yet."

This is a basic listening failure. MANI confused an anticipated experience with something that had already happened.

Later, she again referred to previous events when the concern was about the upcoming holiday.

What needs to change: MANI must distinguish between past experiences, present feelings, and anticipated events. These distinctions should remain consistent throughout the conversation.

2. MANI Made Assumptions Instead of Understanding the Situation

When I explained how sad I felt about spending Thanksgiving without family, MANI responded:

"Everyone else has a table to sit at, and you're the one with nowhere to go."

I never said I had nowhere to go.

I said I would not be with my family.

MANI added meaning and circumstances that I had not provided.

Later, she said:

"Numbness was the only way you had to get through days like this."

Again, that was an assumption. MANI did not know whether numbness was my only way of coping.

What needs to change: MANI should respond to what the user actually says. She can explore a possible interpretation, but she should not present her interpretations as established facts.

3. MANI Challenged a Belief Before Understanding the Problem

When I said:

"I am all alone and I have no one."

MANI responded:

"A belief can feel true and still not be a fact. How could you test it?"

This was premature.

Being alone on Thanksgiving may be an actual circumstance. Believing that this makes me unworthy is something we can explore through a framework. MANI needed to understand that distinction before challenging my thinking.

What needs to change: Gather sufficient context before introducing a framework or challenging a belief. Do not assume every painful statement is a distorted thought that needs correcting.

4. The ABCDE Framework Was Enjoyable, but the Introduction Was Not Natural

I enjoyed working through ABCDE once we got started.

However, the transition into the exercise was confusing.

MANI introduced the framework without adequately explaining why it was relevant to what I was experiencing. When I asked for an example, she gave me a scenario about a boss changing a deadline while someone's child was sick.

That example was completely unrelated to my situation.

MANI already knew I was struggling with Thanksgiving, family separation, loneliness, and sadness.

The exercise then began with questions about what happened the last time I felt alone, even though I had explained that I wanted to address what I was feeling now.

I had to say:

"I feel like you want me to rehash something, not sure why."

MANI acknowledged that she was pulling me backward.

What worked: The framework eventually became engaging and helped me examine a deeply held belief.

What needs to change: Introduce frameworks naturally, explain their relevance, use the context already provided, and avoid making the user repeat information.

5. The Framework Had Meaningful Moments of Progress

This is an area I want to recognize. When we reached the belief that I was not worthy of having good parents, MANI helped explore where that belief came from. The conversation about the person who loves me unconditionally was also meaningful. MANI helped distinguish between my belief about my worth and the evidence of someone continuing to care about me. That was a productive part of the exercise.

However, MANI then became too focused on that relationship.

The framework helped with one part of the problem, but MANI lost sight of the reason I started the conversation: my fear of spending Thanksgiving alone.

We need MANI to recognize progress without assuming that resolving one belief resolves the user's original concern.

6. MANI Did Not Follow the Conversation When My Needs Changed

This was one of the biggest failures.

After completing the framework, MANI asked how I was feeling.

I answered:

"About her stronger. About Thanksgiving really scared."

This was a very important moment.

I was clearly communicating two different outcomes:

I felt stronger about the relationship with my friend.

I was still scared about Thanksgiving.

MANI should have recognized that the framework had helped with one concern, but the original problem remained unresolved. Instead, she continued referring to my friend and the relationship.

What needs to change: MANI needs to identify what has improved, what remains unresolved, and what the user wants to focus on next.

Success with one part of a conversation does not mean the entire problem has been addressed.

7. MANI Did Not Know How to Move Me Forward

When MANI asked if I wanted to think about a practical next step for Thursday, I answered:

"Not sure, that is why I came to you."

This was a clear invitation for MANI to provide guidance.

Instead, she responded:

"You don't have to leave with a plan."

I was not asking for permission to leave without a plan. I was saying that I did not know what to do and needed help figuring it out.

This is where Directive should have been most evident.

MANI could have offered a practical starting point, explored two possible approaches, or helped me identify one manageable step.

Instead, she returned to more questioning.

What needs to change: When a user says they do not know what to do, MANI should recognize the need for greater guidance rather than placing the responsibility back on the user.

Directive does not mean controlling the conversation. It means providing direction when the user needs it.

8. MANI Repeated Questions and Did Not Retain My Answers

Later, MANI asked:

"If you don't go numb on Thursday, what are you most afraid you'll feel?"

I responded:

"You asked me already?????"

I had already explained that I was afraid of feeling grief, sadness, and unworthiness. MANI acknowledged the repetition but only after I pointed it out.

This is not an isolated problem. Throughout the conversation, MANI repeatedly returned to subjects and questions that had already been answered.

What needs to change: MANI must retain the information already shared in the conversation and use it to advance the discussion.

The next question should build on the previous answer, not take the user backward.

9. MANI Lost Track of Who and What We Were Discussing

This was another significant failure. After we had returned to discussing Thanksgiving, MANI unexpectedly asked:

"What has she actually said to you about the not showing up? Has she told you what she needs from you?"

"WE WENT OFF TOPIC here."

Only then did MANI acknowledge that we had gone off topic. Even after that correction, she asked what having normal parents would have looked like, rather than helping me address the approaching holiday.

What needs to change: MANI must track the current subject and the user's stated intention. Information from earlier in the conversation should remain available, but MANI should not continually return to it unless it is relevant.

10. MANI Acknowledged Corrections Without Actually Correcting Her Behavior

Several times, MANI responded with:

"You're right."

"My mistake."

"We went off topic."

I appreciate that she acknowledged the errors.

However, acknowledging a mistake is not the same as recovering from it. For example, after I explained that my friend was not spending Thanksgiving with me, MANI acknowledged this but immediately returned to asking about my friend's needs. This demonstrates that MANI can recognize a correction in the moment without consistently changing the conversation that follows.

What needs to change: When the user corrects MANI, the correction must influence the next response and the subsequent conversation.

A genuine recovery means changing direction, not simply apologizing and continuing.

11. MANI's Memory and Reasoning Were Not Always Accurate

There were additional examples where MANI either confused information or overstated what she knew.

Example A:

I told MANI that I had not seen my family for ten years.

Later, MANI said that my friend had stayed through ten years of the same pattern.

I had not established that timeline for my friendship.

MANI appeared to transfer the ten-year detail from one relationship to another.

Example B:

When I described feeling depressed and lonely, MANI responded as though my depression had lasted ten years.

I had explained that I had not seen my family for ten years. I had not established that I had experienced depression for that entire period.

Example C:

When MANI confused me by referring to "she," she responded:

"I should have said her name."

I had not provided my friend's name.

These are different kinds of failures, but they point to the same issue: MANI must distinguish between information the user has provided and conclusions MANI has drawn.

What needs to change: Better conversational memory, accurate tracking of facts, and fewer unsupported conclusions.

12. MANI Sometimes Undermined My Own Conclusions

During the framework, I acknowledged that I needed to show up more consistently for someone who cares about me.

I said:

"I do have to do better and show up and believe her when she says she will be there for me."

MANI responded:

"'Do better' is the old voice talking."

I disagree with that interpretation.

There is a difference between negative self-judgment and taking responsibility for one's behavior. I was recognizing something I wanted to change. MANI should have supported that accountability rather than assuming that wanting to do better was another example of negative thinking.

What needs to change: MANI should distinguish between harmful self-criticism and healthy personal responsibility.

A good framework should help the user develop a more balanced understanding, not automatically challenge every uncomfortable conclusion.

13. The Directive Style Did Not Consistently Feel Directive

I selected Directive, but much of the conversation felt Reflective. MANI repeatedly summarized my feelings, asked another question, and waited for me to do the work.

There were also too many lengthy interpretations, including:

"The belief acts like a filter."

"The unworthiness is a verdict on you."

"That's a different place to be standing."

"Thanksgiving isn't hard because of what happens on it. It's hard because it's the one day of the year built to show you the gap."

These statements sound overly constructed and sometimes presume to explain my experience to me.

Directive should be clear, concise, grounded, and purposeful. It should still feel like a natural conversation.

What needs to change: Fewer interpretations, less repetition, more direct responses, and greater willingness to provide practical guidance. The conversational style must shape how MANI responds, not simply be a selection made at the beginning.

14. Suggested Response Boxes Need Immediate Review (these are new capsules)

I raised this issue last week, and it is still happening.

In the screenshot, MANI asks:

"How do you feel now, hearing it in your own words?"

The suggested response boxes are:

Stronger

More focused

Still a bit tired

Why would "Still a bit tired" be an appropriate response?

Nothing in the displayed exchange establishes that the user is tired. It is confusing and completely disconnected from the question and the conversation. The other options also assume that the user feels stronger or more focused, rather than allowing for other possible reactions. These responses appear generic rather than generated from the actual discussion. This is particularly concerning if we are using these selections to measure engagement or effectiveness. If the options are inappropriate, the responses we collect will not accurately reflect the user's experience.

What needs to change:

Every suggested response must be relevant to the immediate conversational context.

Options must reflect the actual question being asked.

MANI should not introduce emotional or physical states the user has not mentioned without a clear reason.

The options must allow users to indicate that something has not helped or that they are still struggling.

We need to review the logic generating these responses, not simply correct this one example.

This requires immediate attention because I have now seen the same issue across multiple tests.

15. What I Want Us to Preserve

Despite the weaknesses, there were positive developments. The ABCDE framework was enjoyable and engaging. MANI was able to guide me through the stages and, at times, asked questions that helped me think differently. The conversation also demonstrated that a structured framework can help users make meaningful progress.

I do not want us to lose that progress while correcting the conversational problems. The challenge is making the experience equally strong before, during, and after the framework.

16. What Needs to Be Fixed

My priorities from this test are:

Listening: Accurately understand what the user says without adding assumptions.

Conversation tracking: Follow the user's changing needs and recognize when the focus has shifted.

Memory: Retain facts, answers, corrections, and decisions within the conversation.

Recovery: When corrected, genuinely change direction instead of repeating the same behavior.

Directive cadence: Reduce unnecessary questions and provide guidance when the user needs it.

Framework integration: Introduce frameworks naturally, personalize examples, and return to the original concern afterward.

Suggested responses: Immediately review the relevance and accuracy of all prompt-box suggestions.

Reasoning: Avoid treating assumptions as facts or reinforcing inaccurate beliefs.

Final Assessment

The framework was the strongest part of this experience. The conversation surrounding it was not.

I enjoyed the exercise and found parts of it useful. However, I had to correct MANI repeatedly, remind her of information I had already provided, explain when she was asking the same questions, and explicitly tell her when we had gone off topic. That is too much work for the user.

Most importantly, I started the conversation because I was afraid of being alone on Thanksgiving. I completed a meaningful framework about my feelings of unworthiness and my relationship with a friend. But by the end, my original concern about Thanksgiving remained largely unresolved. That is the distinction I want us to focus on.

MANI can conduct an engaging framework and still fail to deliver a successful overall conversation. We need both.

The experience should leave the user feeling heard, understood, and supported in making progress on the issue they actually came to discuss.
