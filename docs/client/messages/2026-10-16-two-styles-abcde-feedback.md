### 5. The biggest framework failure: MANI treats ABCDE as a questionnaire

This happens in both conversations.

A: Ask question. Receive answer. Move on.

B: Ask question. Receive answer. Move on.

C: Ask question. Receive answer. Move on.

D: Ask question. Receive answer. Move on.

E: Ask question. Receive answer. Move on.

This is the fundamental problem.

MANI is completing a sequence rather than facilitating understanding.

The framework needs to function as a guided conversation.

That means MANI must recognize when an answer is incomplete, explore it further, clarify what the person means, and help them reach an understanding before progressing.

The goal is not to complete five letters.

The goal is to help the person make a meaningful change in how they understand their situation.

For example:

"A stands for Activating Event. We've identified that you're facing a long list of unfinished responsibilities. Is that the situation you'd like us to work through?"

That would make the framework feel connected to the conversation rather than like a new interview.

B: Belief
The user said:

"That I won't get it all done there's just too much and so I dont start."

The belief: "I won't get it all done. There's too much."

The behavioral response: "So I don't start."

MANI should distinguish between them.

A stronger response would identify the belief and explore whether it contains an assumption that everything must be completed before any progress counts.

Then MANI moved on.

The user had initially described anxiety, overwhelm, frustration, and an inability to act.

The consequences should include emotional and behavioral effects, not simply confirmation that the person did not begin their tasks.

MANI missed the opportunity to connect the person's belief to what they were feeling and doing.

D: Dispute
This is where the framework begins to fail most seriously.

The user said: "Nothing comes to mind for me."

Both versions responded by asking whether the person had enough information to know that they would not get everything done.

The user answered no.

But saying "I don't know for sure" is not the same as challenging a belief in a meaningful way.

The person might genuinely have more responsibilities than they can complete.

The goal should not be to persuade them that their belief is false.

The goal should be to examine the belief carefully.

For example, MANI might explore the difference between:

"I cannot get everything done."

and

"If I cannot get everything done, there is no point in starting."

Those are different beliefs with different consequences.

That distinction could have produced an actual insight.

Instead, MANI settled for a logical technicality.

Most important

Create Meaningful Conversation Within Each Stage of the Framework.

MANI should not simply ask a question, receive an answer, verify or confirm it, and move on to the next letter. Each stage of the framework needs to be a natural, engaging conversation with more back-and-forth discussion, exploration, and deeper understanding.

MANI should stay with the person's responses, ask thoughtful follow-up questions, explore what they mean, help them recognize patterns, and work through their thinking together. Some stages may require several exchanges before the person reaches a meaningful understanding. The goal is not to rush through A, B, C, D, and E, but to make each stage a valuable part of the conversation.

The person should come away feeling they learned something new about themselves, experienced an "aha" moment, gained a different perspective, and developed a clearer understanding of how to move forward. The framework should feel like a thoughtful, human conversation, not a questionnaire.

### 7. Additional issues I would raise

First: The system confuses an answer with completion.

A short response such as "yes," "no," or "nothing comes to mind" does not necessarily mean the person has understood or completed the stage.

MANI needs to evaluate the substance of the answer, not simply whether an answer was provided.

Second: MANI does not sufficiently recognize when someone needs help thinking.

When the user says "Nothing comes to mind" or "not sure," that is precisely when the framework should become more helpful.

MANI should offer a thoughtful distinction, an example, or a relevant question. It should not require the person to generate every insight independently.

Third: MANI misses the opportunity to connect the framework stages.

A, B, C, D, and E should build upon one another.

For example, the belief identified in B should be the belief examined in D and reconsidered in E.

The current implementation behaves as though each letter is a separate question.

Fourth: The system needs to recognize when a proposed reframe is not accepted.

When the person rejects an interpretation, MANI must not treat that as an acceptable outcome and move forward.

It needs to understand why the interpretation did not help and continue working with the person.

Fifth: MANI needs a stronger distinction between completing a framework and abandoning it.

A user can decline to continue at any time. But when the person is engaged and simply cannot find an answer, MANI should not silently end the framework.

Sixth: Both styles contain excessive procedural language.

Reflective repeatedly says things like "I want to understand" and "I don't want to guess."

Directive also uses "I want to understand" and similar statements.

These expressions can be appropriate occasionally, but repeated use makes MANI sound scripted. It should demonstrate understanding through the relevance of its responses, rather than repeatedly announcing its intention to understand.

Seventh: The somatic transition is premature in both conversations.

Neither conversation reaches a meaningful completion of E.

Nevertheless, both proceed to the body check.

Somatic support should follow the framework at the appropriate point, not function as an automatic escape when the framework becomes difficult.

Eighth: The response to "my eyes are burning" needs attention.

Reflective interpreted this as a reason not to do breathing and offered a general question.

Directive instructed the user to focus on the sensation and breathe out.

Neither established what "my eyes are burning" meant.

It could describe physical irritation, exhaustion, or the onset of tears. MANI should not automatically interpret it as emotional tension or assume that directing attention to the eyes is appropriate.

### 8. What the user should have experienced

At the end of ABCDE, the person should ideally be able to recognize something like this:

"I have been treating the fact that I cannot finish everything as a reason not to start anything."

That could lead to a more useful understanding:

"I may not be able to finish everything, but I can still make progress on something."

This is only an illustration, not a conclusion MANI should force onto the person.

The difference is significant.

The first belief connects an overwhelming workload to paralysis.

The second acknowledges the workload while allowing for action.

That is the kind of shift ABCDE is intended to support.

It offers the possibility of an "aha" moment because the person recognizes something about their thinking that they had not fully understood before.

And importantly, the person arrives at that understanding through conversation, rather than having a replacement belief handed to them.
